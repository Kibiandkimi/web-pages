Uphill Both Ways

What 25 GPS tracks reveal about getting around campus: road by road, in both directions, on foot and by bike.

English PresentationNovember 2026
walking flowcycling flowdots move at the model's speed for each road and direction
Good morning. Like many of you, I spend a lot of time getting from one end of our campus to the other. I recorded my own GPS tracks and used them to rebuild how fast you can actually travel on every road here, in both directions. The moving dots behind me are simulated walkers and cyclists, each moving at the speed my model predicts for that road and direction.
01 · The question

A break between classes. Is the trip from A to B the same as from B to A?

Direction

How far is "close"?

How much of campus can you reach in 5, 10 or 15 minutes from where you live?

Our breaks are 5, 10 or 20 minutes. Huiwen to Zhili on foot takes about 20 minutes, plus a minute to get in and out of buildings, so it doesn't even fit the long break. That raised three questions: does direction matter, which trips actually fit, and how far is "close"?

The data: 25 tracks I recorded myself, about 48 kilometres in total. Each track is matched to OpenStreetMap roads with a hidden Markov model; 88 to 100 percent of points match, usually within a few metres. On the map, blue is road I walked and orange is road I cycled. Everything else is filled in by the model.
03 · Method

From raw GPS to a directed model of campus

Six steps. Upload the track and detect walking, running or cycling. Match every point to a road and a direction. Add the slope from a shared terrain model. Fit a robust regression that separates my personal pace from slope, road type and rush hour. Then plan routes on a directed graph, where A to B and B to A are separate edges, and finally test it on tracks the model has never seen.
04 · Terrain

The campus is a hill

Huiwen sits at about 33 m. The southern teaching cluster (Zhili, Zhizhen, Zhixin) sits at 5–8 m. Every trip between them climbs or drops about 25 m.

2 m35 melevation · contours every 2 m

Terrain: DirMap shared track-height model, 10 m grid. Slope is integrated every ~10 m and per direction, so a dip followed by a climb never cancels out to "flat".

This is why direction matters. The terrain comes from a shared elevation model built from many GPS tracks. The north of campus, around Huiwen, is about 25 metres higher than the southern teaching buildings. Contours are every 2 metres.

Road type matters even more than slope for bikes. On footways a bike runs at about 77 percent of road speed because you share them with pedestrians. On footbridges it's half, and on steps you push, which takes six and a half times as long. For walkers, roads, footways and bridges are all the same. Only steps slow you down.

Rush hour. When classes change, cycling on shared paths takes about 26 percent longer. Walking doesn't change measurably. I deliberately did not model a peak effect on roads: when I'm late I simply ride faster, and the data can't tell that apart from traffic. An earlier version claimed roads were faster at peak, which was exactly this artefact.

How accurate is it? I left each track out, refit the model, and predicted that trip. Walking: about 10 percent error. Cycling: 23 percent, only a little better than assuming a constant speed. But if you know how fast the first 40 percent of the ride went, the error drops to 14 percent, so most of the error is simply how fast I happened to be riding that day.
15 · Takeaways

What the tracks taught me

1

Direction is real, for bikes

Crossing the hill costs a cyclist 13–19% extra (about a minute) going north. Walkers hardly notice.

2

Steps and bridges dominate cycling

A single flight of steps can outweigh a long detour. The best bike route is often not the shortest.

3

The break, not the distance, is the constraint

Huiwen to the south cluster doesn't fit a 20-minute break on foot. With a bike, every pair does.

Limitations
  • One person, 25 tracks, few peak-hour rides. Most roads fall back to their road-type average.
  • GPS can't separate paths only a few metres apart.
  • The slope effect is a correlation after adjusting for pace, not proof of cause.
  • Places are building-group centroids: real doors and bike parking add tens of seconds.
Three takeaways. Direction is real, but mostly for bikes. For cyclists, steps and footbridges matter more than distance. And the real constraint is the break length: some walking trips simply don't fit. The limitations matter too: this is one person's data, so treat the numbers as estimates, not laws.
