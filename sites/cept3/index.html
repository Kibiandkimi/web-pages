Walk,rideor wait?

The pros and cons of every way to get to class — measured on the ground, not guessed.

WalkBikeCampus shuttle

81 GPS tracks · 38 stopwatch trips · 180 minutes of live bus positions · data run 22 Nov 2026

Good morning, everyone. Every day on this campus we make the same small decision: do I walk, take a bike, or wait for the shuttle? Most of us decide by habit. We decided to measure instead. The moving dots behind me are real routes on the real road network. I will show the advantages and disadvantages of each choice, and finish with advice for students and for the university.

Let us start with a race. Three students leave Qiao Dorms together, all going to Huiwen, on top of the hill. The cyclist arrives in about six and a half minutes. The shuttle passenger walks to the stop, waits about four minutes, rides for four and walks again: twelve and a half minutes. The walker needs eighteen. And this is the shuttle's best route on the whole campus. So when is each choice actually the right one?

Huiwen ↔ Zhili by bike

A 27 m climb: Huiwen stands at 33.6 m, Zhili at 8.6 m. On foot the two directions take 19.0 and 19.2 min. The map behind shows the height model.

But the bike has weaknesses. The first is hills. The amber line, walking, is almost flat. The teal line, cycling, is steep. On a five percent uphill a cyclist needs thirty percent more time; a walker only five. Downhill the cyclist gets about twenty percent back. You can feel it between Huiwen and Zhili: five point six minutes down, six and a half back up. So if your class is on the hill, leave one minute earlier.

On a whole trip that is small: +10% at the median, never more than 1.3 min.

FORFastest everywhere; all of campus within 10 min.AGAINSTHills, steps, crowds — and you must find, unlock and park it (not measured).
The second weakness is anything that makes you get off the bike. Steps cost a walker about twice the normal time, but a cyclist six times, because you push. A footbridge slows a bike by seventy percent. At the bell, crowded footpaths slow a bike by a third, while walkers do not slow down at all. Still, on a whole trip the bell costs about one minute at most. The bike stays fastest if you stay on the roads.

The one trip that beat walking: going to the stop 10 minutes early.

FORNo effort, no hill; quick once you are on board.AGAINSTYou cannot know the wait, and the stops are far from most doors.

Stopwatch: one person, 7 shuttle trips. Tracker: 180 min in two windows; Sunday midday only 2 buses (10.1-min gap). Some buses carry no tracker, so real gaps may be shorter.

So what happens with the wait? My classmate tried it. Red dots are shuttle trips; amber dots are walks on the same route. Six of seven shuttle trips were slower than simply walking. The only winner needed going to the stop ten minutes early. The tracker says a bus comes every five or six minutes, so about a four-minute wait. The stopwatch suggests seven to fourteen. It is a small sample, but the lesson is clear: the ride is short, and the wait is what you cannot predict.

Distance picks the mode: walk the short trips, ride the rest

Median minutes for all 380 trips, grouped by walking distance. Shuttle includes the average wait; “wins” = beats walking. Bike overhead of 2 min is an assumption, not a measurement.

By distance we get a simple rule. Short trips on the left, long trips on the right. Under three hundred metres the bike saves only a minute and a half. If unlocking and parking take two minutes — our assumption, not a measurement — walking is just as fast. Below six hundred metres the shuttle never beats walking. Only beyond one point two kilometres does it win about half the time. And it never beats the bike.

Here is the whole comparison in one table. Walking is slow but perfectly predictable, with no waiting and no trouble with hills or the bell. The bike is the fastest and still predictable, but hills, steps and crowded footpaths hurt it, and you have to park it. The shuttle climbs the hill for you, but the waiting is unpredictable and the stops are far from most doors. Comfort in rain or with a heavy bag is probably the shuttle's real strength — we did not measure it.

For students: four rules the data supports

Walk
under about 300 m, or next door

The bike saves less than 2 min — before you unlock it. Zhili ↔ Zhizhen: 3.4 min on foot.

Ride
across the hill, or beyond about 1 km

Huiwen → Zhili: 19.0 min on foot, 5.6 by bike. Uphill, leave 1 min earlier. At the bell, stay on roads.

Shuttle
only with a stop near both doors

Qiao or Haishan ↔ Huiwen and Sci-Tech. No bus after your break-even wait (≈10 min for Qiao → Huiwen)? Walk. From South Dorms: never.

Always
add 20% to any estimate

That margin covers 86% of the 69 commutes we recorded (59 of 69).

Rain, heat, a heavy bag or an injury can still justify the shuttle — comfort was not measured.

So what should a student do? Four rules. One: under about three hundred metres, walk. [Click] Two: across the hill, or beyond a kilometre, ride. Uphill, leave a minute earlier, and at the bell use the roads. [Click] Three: take the shuttle only when a stop is near both doors. If no bus has come after your break-even wait, start walking. [Click] Four: whatever you choose, add twenty percent. That covers eighty-six percent of our real commutes.

I should be honest about the limits. This is very few people: one student's GPS, one classmate's stopwatch, only seven shuttle trips, and no weekday morning rush. We travelled only a small part of the path network; the rest is estimated from road type and slope. And some things we did not measure at all: parking a bike, full buses, weather, cost, safety, e-bikes. So please treat these as careful estimates, not exact facts.

To finish, the whole talk in one breath. Walk the short ones. Ride the hill. And wait for the shuttle only if the stop is at your door. The bike is two point seven times faster than walking. The shuttle beats walking on three routes out of twenty-four, and the bike on none. So tomorrow, before you join the queue at the bus stop, ask how far you are really going.
