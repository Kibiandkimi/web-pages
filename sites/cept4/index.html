Walk, cycle
or shuttle?

The pros and cons of every way to get to class, measured on the ground.

Walk Bike Campus shuttle

English class presentation · 81 GPS tracks, 38 stopwatch trips, 180 minutes of bus positions

Hello, everyone. Every day on this campus we make the same small decision: do I walk, take a bike, or wait for the shuttle bus?

Most of us decide by habit. We decided to measure instead. I will show the real advantages and disadvantages of each choice, and finish with advice for students and for the university.

Let us start with a race. Three students leave Qiao Dorms together, all going to Huiwen, on top of the hill.

The cyclist arrives in about six and a half minutes. The walker needs eighteen. The shuttle passenger walks to the stop, waits about four minutes, rides for four, and walks again: twelve and a half minutes.

And this is the shuttle's best route from any dorm to any teaching building. So when is each choice actually the right one?

But the bike has weaknesses. The first is hills. The amber line, walking, is almost flat. The teal line, cycling, is steep.

On a five percent uphill, a cyclist needs thirty percent more time; a walker, only five. Downhill, the cyclist gets about twenty percent back.

You can feel it between Huiwen and Zhili: five point six minutes down, six and a half back up. So if your class is on the hill, leave one minute earlier.

Cycling · the catch

Steps, footbridges and the bell slow a cyclist far more than a walker

  • 49 flights of steps and 69 footbridge sections in the campus core — on a bike you push.
  • At the bell, shared footpaths cost a cyclist ×1.34; roads cost nothing extra.
  • On a whole trip that is small: +10 % at the median, never more than 1.3 min.

For Fastest on every route; all of campus within 10 min.

Against Hills, steps, crowds — and you must find, unlock and park it (not measured).

The second weakness is anything that makes you get off the bike.

Steps cost a walker about twice the normal time, but a cyclist six times, because you push. A footbridge slows a bike by seventy percent. And at the bell, crowded footpaths slow a bike by a third, while walkers do not slow down at all.

Still, on a whole trip the bell costs about one minute at most. The bike stays fastest, if you stay on the roads.

Campus shuttle · the wait

6 of 7 timed shuttle trips were slower than walking the same route

  • Bus tracker, Tuesday afternoon: a bus every 5.6–5.8 min at the median, worst gap 20.5 min.
  • That implies an average wait of 4.1 min. The stopwatch implies 7.5–14 min (3 trips).
  • The one trip that beat walking: going to the stop 10 minutes early.

For No effort, no hill; quick once you are on board.

Against You cannot know the wait, and the stops are far from most doors.

Stopwatch: one person, 7 shuttle trips. Tracker: 180 min in two windows; some buses carry no tracker, so real gaps may be shorter.

So what happens with the wait? My classmate tried it. Violet dots are shuttle trips; amber dots are walks on the same route.

Six of seven shuttle trips were slower than simply walking. The one trip that beat walking meant going to the stop ten minutes early.

The tracker says a bus comes every five or six minutes, so about a four-minute wait. The stopwatch suggests seven to fourteen. It is a small sample, but the lesson is clear: the ride is short, and the wait is what you cannot predict.

By distance, we get a simple rule. Short trips are on the left, long trips on the right.

Under three hundred metres, the bike saves less than two minutes. If unlocking and parking take two minutes — that is our assumption, not a measurement — walking is just as fast.

Below six hundred metres, the shuttle never beats walking. Only beyond one point two kilometres does it beat walking about half the time. And it never beats the bike.

Rain, heat, a heavy bag or an injury can still justify the shuttle — comfort was not measured.

So what should a student do? Four rules.

One: under about three hundred metres, walk.

Two: across the hill, or beyond a kilometre, ride. Uphill, leave a minute earlier, and at the bell use the roads.

Three: take the shuttle only when a stop is near both doors. Even on the best route, it is worth a wait of ten minutes at most; if the wait looks longer, walk.

Four: whatever you choose, add twenty percent. And of course, in heavy rain, comfort matters more than minutes.

Recommendations · university and shuttle operator

For the university: fix the wait, the gap and the steps

  1. 20.5min worst gap
    Put a tracker on every bus and a countdown at every stop. The ride is 1–6 min; the uncertainty is the wait.
  2. 13–14min implied waits
    Add short runs between Qiao / Haishan and Huiwen at class change. This is the one corridor where the shuttle wins.
  3. 16 of 33passes detoured
    Skip the North Gate detour when nobody needs it. It adds about 5.4 min each time.
  4. 9.2min walk to a stop
    Give South Dorms a stop, or a shortcut to one. Today the shuttle is useless there.
  5. ×6.1on steps by bike

And the university? Five ideas, each tied to a number.

One: a tracker on every bus and a countdown at the stops, because the problem is not knowing the wait.

Two: extra short runs between Qiao, Haishan and Huiwen at class change, where the shuttle really helps.

Three: skip the North Gate detour when nobody needs it; it costs five minutes.

Four: South Dorms need a stop. And five, for cyclists: ramps beside steps, bike lanes, and parking at the doors.

Limitations

What these numbers cannot tell you

Few people, few trips

One student’s GPS, so one person’s pace. One classmate’s stopwatch: 7 shuttle trips. 180 minutes of bus positions, and no weekday morning rush.

Most paths were never travelled

The rest is estimated from road type and slope. Slopes steeper than ±4 % are extrapolated.

Not measured at all

Time to find, unlock and park a bike (the 2 min is assumed). Full buses. Weather, cost, comfort, safety. There is no e-bike data.

The tracker misses some buses, so real waits could be shorter than it suggests — but the stopwatch trips suggest they were longer.

I should be honest about the limits. This is very few people: one student's GPS, one classmate's stopwatch, only seven shuttle trips, and no weekday morning rush.

We travelled only a small part of the path network; the rest is estimated from road type and slope.

And some things we did not measure at all: parking a bike, full buses, weather, cost, safety, e-bikes. So please treat these as careful estimates, not exact facts.

To finish, the whole talk in one breath. Walk the short ones. Ride the hill. And wait for the shuttle only if the stop is at your door.

The bike is two point seven times faster than walking. The shuttle beats walking on three routes out of twenty-four, and the bike on none.

So tomorrow, before you join the queue at the bus stop, ask how far you are really going. Thank you — I am happy to take questions.
